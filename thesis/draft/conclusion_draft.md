This thesis asked whether the collision avoidance supplied by an inference-time control-barrier-function (CBF) shield can be amortized into a vision-language-action policy, so that the shield need not run at deployment. In the SafeLIBERO setting studied here, the answer is yes, but only under a specific form of supervision. Distilling $\pi_{0.5}$ on rollouts corrected by its own CBF shield reduced collision episodes from 82.5% to 19.2% **without running the shield at inference**, while increasing task success from 58.3% to 82.5% on held-out initial states. The resulting policy achieved 80.8% collision-avoidance rate, approaching the shielded policy's 86.7%, and one distillation round was sufficient.

This result extends the question posed by AEGIS  [CITE: aegis2025 — cite their *setting and method*: a perception-grounded vision-language safety assessment feeding a CBF-QP that modifies $\pi_{0.5}$'s nominal actions at inference, and the SafeLIBERO benchmark itself. VERIFIED: their Table I reports CAR 77.9%/68.9% against $\pi_{0.5}$'s 18.7%/17.1%, and their QP runs every step at 0.356 ms]. That work improves $\pi_{0.5}$ on SafeLIBERO by *retaining* the constraint layer at inference; the present work asks what remains when its corrective behaviour is instead used as training data and the layer is removed. What is demonstrated is an empirical reduction in collisions, not a transfer of the CBF's formal safety guarantee: the student is no longer protected by the online constraint satisfaction that establishes forward invariance  [CITE: cbf_theory — cite the *definition*: safety as forward invariance of a safe set enforced through an input inequality, which is the property the student loses. And CITE: il_with_cbf — cite their *conditions*: input-to-state safety is inherited by a learned controller only under explicit robustness and learning assumptions, none of which this experiment establishes].

The central boundary condition concerns the source and location of correction. A safe-by-construction, geometry-privileged sampling-based planner did not improve the same student under a comparison matched for gradient steps, hyperparameters, and evaluation. Conversely, filtering only for successful episodes also produced no measurable gain. Taken together, these controls support the hypothesis that transferable supervision must correct errors on, or near, the learner's own rollout distribution; mere access to successful trajectories — even from a stronger controller — is insufficient in this setting. Cite their *ablation* (their Table 2, VR-UnseenTable OOD, 3 seeds): replacing that action-space teacher with text-based privileged context collapses success to 75.8% for MCTS PI — 11.4 points below the PPO baseline of 87.2% — and to 4.68% for both RelSpatial and Plan+RelSpatial PI, against 91.5% for the full method. The relationship is worth stating precisely, because it is a gap rather than a disagreement: every teacher ROAD-VLA rejects is a *textual* one, and every teacher it accepts is derived from the student itself, so no foreign low-level controller appears anywhere in that work. Its proximity principle therefore predicts the planner result reported here, while the experiment that would confirm it — a privileged controller supplying well-formed low-level actions from outside the policy — is the one this thesis contributes.

The remaining ablations sharpen, rather than universalise, that claim. The distilled student avoided collisions involving arm links more effectively than its teacher, indicating that trajectory imitation alone does not fully describe the learned behaviour  [CITE: aegis2025 — cite their *stated limitation* that their formulation "solely constrains the end-effector", so unconstrained kinematic links may collide. VERIFIED. The barrier used here additionally constrains links 3–7 and the hand, which is why arm-link collisions are measurable at all in this comparison]. Scalar-reward reinforcement learning, guided sampling, language-specified safety, and best-of-$N$ action selection did not provide an effective substitute under the tested implementations and budgets. The reinforcement-learning negative is a result about the reward design tested, not a rejection of safe RL as a class  [CITE: guidedbyguardrails — cite their *argument* that unterminated continuous negative rewards accumulate in unsafe regions and create a learning barrier, which is the failure mode observed here. CITE: safedojo — cite it as *scope control*: a model-based safe-RL method for VLAs using action-conditioned video world models and Lagrangian constrained GRPO, evaluated on SafeLIBERO, and therefore a materially richer signal than the scalar reward tested here. CITE: safevla — cite their *constrained-MDP formulation* as the second such alternative]. Best-of-$N$ likewise found little useful variation among samples drawn from the same state; this supports a state-dominant account of collision risk **in this policy and benchmark**, rather than a general claim about generative policies.

The practical conclusion is therefore narrower than "safety can be learned." A runtime filter can teach a policy the regularities that it observes and corrects, and policy-proximal corrections can amortize a substantial portion of that benefit — without the per-step optimization that comparable methods retain at inference  [CITE: conbat — cite their *deployment step*: lightweight online optimization selecting actions whose predicted future states stay in a learned safe set. CSV, VERIFY. CITE: latentpolicybarrier — cite their *inference-time latent steering* back toward the expert manifold. CSV, VERIFY. Both learn safety representations but keep a runtime correction; the distinction claimed here is removal of that step, not learning the barrier]. But the learned policy is only as broad as the states, hazards, and geometry represented in those corrections. Runtime shielding remains necessary outside that validated envelope; distillation reduces dependence on the shield, not the need for a safety case.
