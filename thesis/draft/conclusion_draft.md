This thesis asked whether the collision avoidance supplied by an inference-time control-barrier-function (CBF) shield can be amortized into a vision-language-action policy, so that the shield need not run at deployment. In the SafeLIBERO setting studied here, the answer is yes, but only under a specific form of supervision. Distilling $\pi_{0.5}$ on rollouts corrected by its own CBF shield reduced collision episodes from 82.5% to 19.2% **without running the shield at inference**, while increasing task success from 58.3% to 82.5% on held-out initial states. The resulting policy achieved 80.8% collision-avoidance rate, approaching the shielded policy's 86.7%, and one distillation round was sufficient.

This result extends the question posed by AEGIS  [CITE: aegis2025 — cite their *setting and method*: a perception-grounded vision-language safety assessment feeding a CBF-QP that modifies $\pi_{0.5}$'s nominal actions at inference, and the SafeLIBERO benchmark itself. VERIFIED: their Table I reports CAR 77.9%/68.9% against $\pi_{0.5}$'s 18.7%/17.1%, and their QP runs every step at 0.356 ms]. That work improves $\pi_{0.5}$ on SafeLIBERO by *retaining* the constraint layer at inference; the present work asks what remains when its corrective behaviour is instead used as training data and the layer is removed. What is demonstrated is an empirical reduction in collisions, not a transfer of the CBF's formal safety guarantee: the student is no longer protected by the online constraint satisfaction that establishes forward invariance  [CITE: cbf_theory — cite the *definition*: safety as forward invariance of a safe set enforced through an input inequality, which is the property the student loses. And CITE: il_with_cbf — cite their *conditions*: input-to-state safety is inherited by a learned controller only under explicit robustness and learning assumptions, none of which this experiment establishes].

The central boundary condition concerns the source and location of correction. A safe-by-construction, geometry-privileged sampling-based planner did not improve the same student under a comparison matched for gradient steps, hyperparameters, and evaluation. Conversely, filtering only for successful episodes also produced no measurable gain. Taken together, these controls support a hypothesis about the *states* supervision covers rather than about who produces it: transferable supervision must correct errors on, or near, the states the learner itself reaches when it begins to fail. Access to successful trajectories, even trajectories that are safe by construction, is insufficient when they are drawn from a distribution the student does not visit.

The distinction matters because the stronger claim — that a foreign controller cannot teach safety — is false, and the literature already shows it. SAFE-GIL trains behaviour cloning on demonstrations from an MPC or PID expert, neither of which is the learner, and obtains substantially safer policies; what makes those demonstrations work is that adversarial disturbance is injected during collection so that the fixed expert is forced to demonstrate recovery from the safety-critical states a learner's errors would produce  [CITE: safegil — VERIFIED against the PDF. Cite their *method*: Hamilton-Jacobi reachability computes the disturbance that maximally steers the system toward the failure set (their Eq. 4), injected into the expert's action during collection (their Eq. 5, Algorithm 1), so the expert is guided into safety-critical states and its recovery behaviour is recorded. Cite their *result*: collision rate falls sharply against vanilla BC, most in low-data regimes, across 2D navigation, X-Plane aircraft taxiing and a quadrotor. Cite their *ablation* (their Table I) that adversarial disturbance is what matters — Gaussian noise, uniform noise and DART all fail to improve safety. Note the *scale caveat*: MLP policies on low-dimensional state or single-camera input, not a VLA]. Their expert is privileged and foreign; the demonstrations still transfer. The planner tested here differs precisely in that its trajectories were generated from its own initial conditions and never conditioned on where $\pi_{0.5}$ goes wrong, which is why the shielded rollouts — corrections applied at states the policy actually reached — succeeded where it did not. The operative variable is state coverage, not the identity of the teacher.

This interpretation aligns with a second, independent construction of the same idea. IntervenGen rolls out the *current* policy in new scene configurations so that the mistakes it records are the policy's genuine failures rather than replayed ones, detects each mistake, and then transforms a *human* recovery segment onto that state  [CITE: intervengen — VERIFIED against the PDF. Cite their *method*: closed-loop policy execution for mistake generation, contact-triggered mistake detection, and open-loop replay of an SE(3)-transformed human recovery segment, with generated demonstrations kept only if they complete the task. Cite their *result*: up to 39x robustness from ten human interventions, and a policy trained on data synthesised from ten interventions outperforming one trained on a hundred full interventions by 24% at 12% of the collection time. Note two *scope limits* that favour the method here: their Assumptions 1-3 require a delta-pose action space, a known sequence of object-centric subtasks, and object poses observable at each subtask boundary, none of which the shield requires; and their distribution shift originates in object-pose estimation error, so their claim is about robustness to perception error rather than collision safety]. Their recovery actions, like SAFE-GIL's, come from outside the policy — from a human — while the states they annotate come from the policy's own execution. Two independent methods, with different teachers and different means of reaching the failure states, therefore locate the active ingredient in the same place, and neither supports the stronger reading that the teacher must be the policy itself.

The remaining alignment is with the policy-proximal self-distillation of ROAD-VLA, whose design principle anticipates this result without testing it  [CITE: roadvla — VERIFIED against the PDF. Cite their *method*: the teacher is a logit-perturbed copy of the student itself (their Eq. 10), constructed by nudging action-token logits with calibrated advantage estimates, and they derive a policy-improvement bound that holds only while the teacher stays proximal to the current policy. Cite their *ablation* (their Table 2, VR-UnseenTable OOD, 3 seeds): replacing that action-space teacher with text-based privileged context collapses success to 75.8% for MCTS PI — 11.4 points below the PPO baseline of 87.2% — and to 4.68% for both RelSpatial and Plan+RelSpatial PI, against 91.5% for the full method. Cite their *explanation*, which supports the language-channel negative reported in Section 4.7: "post-training VLA weakens the general reasoning of the LLM backbone and ... a modality gap prevents the discrete text from providing the precise grounding required for continuous control."]. The relationship to ROAD-VLA is worth stating precisely, because it is a gap rather than a disagreement: every teacher it rejects is a *textual* one, and every teacher it accepts is derived from the student itself, so no foreign low-level controller appears anywhere in that work. Its proximity principle therefore predicts the planner result reported here, while the experiment that would confirm it — a privileged controller supplying well-formed low-level actions from outside the policy — is the one this thesis contributes.

Two features of the present result are not anticipated by that literature. Where SAFE-GIL reports a safety-performance tradeoff, its own ablation showing task cost rising monotonically as the disturbance bound is raised and more of the training data moves into unsafe states, the distillation reported here improved collision avoidance and task success *together* — 82.5% to 19.2% and 58.3% to 82.5%. And where SAFE-GIL must synthesise the safety-critical states adversarially, because its expert would not otherwise visit them, the shield supplies corrections at states the policy reaches unprompted, so no disturbance model is required. Both differences follow from correcting a policy on its own rollouts rather than steering a foreign expert into the policy's likely failures.

The remaining ablations sharpen, rather than universalise, that claim. The distilled student avoided collisions involving arm links more effectively than its teacher, indicating that trajectory imitation alone does not fully describe the learned behaviour  [CITE: aegis2025 — cite their *stated limitation* that their formulation "solely constrains the end-effector", so unconstrained kinematic links may collide. Scalar-reward reinforcement learning, guided sampling, language-specified safety, and best-of-$N$ action selection did not provide an effective substitute under the tested implementations and budgets. The reinforcement-learning negative is a result about the reward design tested, not a rejection of safe RL as a class  [CITE: guidedbyguardrails — VERIFIED against the PDF. Cite separately, and importantly, their *CBF Filter negative* — training under an active shield and then removing it leaves the agent colliding, with high critic value inside the obstacle — which shows that shield exposure alone does not transfer safety and that the imitation objective is what does the work here. Cite their *CBF Decay* variant as a non-imitation route to the same internalisation. CITE: safedojo — cite it as *scope control*: a model-based safe-RL method for VLAs using action-conditioned video world models and Lagrangian constrained GRPO, evaluated on SafeLIBERO, and therefore a materially richer signal than the scalar reward tested here. CITE: safevla — cite their *constrained-MDP formulation* as the second such alternative]. Best-of-$N$ likewise found little useful variation among samples drawn from the same state; this supports a state-dominant account of collision risk **in this policy and benchmark**, rather than a general claim about generative policies.

The practical conclusion is therefore narrower than "safety can be learned." A runtime filter can teach a policy the regularities that it observes and corrects, and policy-proximal corrections can amortize a substantial portion of that benefit — without the per-step optimization that comparable methods retain at inference  [CITE: conbat — cite their *deployment step*: lightweight online optimization selecting actions whose predicted future states stay in a learned safe set. CSV, VERIFY. CITE: latentpolicybarrier — cite their *inference-time latent steering* back toward the expert manifold. CSV, VERIFY. Both learn safety representations but keep a runtime correction; the distinction claimed here is removal of that step, not learning the barrier]. But the learned policy is only as broad as the states, hazards, and geometry represented in those corrections. Runtime shielding remains necessary outside that validated envelope; distillation reduces dependence on the shield, not the need for a safety case.
